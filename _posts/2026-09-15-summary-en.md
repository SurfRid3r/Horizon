## [Bruce Schneier: 25 Years of Post-9/11 Mass Surveillance Is Enough](https://www.schneier.com/blog/archives/2026/09/25-years-of-mass-surveillance-is-enough.html) ⭐️ 8.0/10

Renowned security expert Bruce Schneier published an essay marking the 25th anniversary of the post-9/11 surveillance era, arguing that a quarter century of mass surveillance has failed to justify its costs and should be brought to an end. The piece offers a critical retrospective on how warrantless programs and expanded legal authorities have eroded privacy and civil liberties since 2001. Schneier is one of the most respected voices in cryptography and security policy, and his call comes as US surveillance law — notably FISA Section 702 — faces repeated reauthorization fights in Congress. The essay reinvigorates the debate over whether the security-versus-civil-liberties trade-off made after 9/11 remains justified a quarter century later, and it drew outsized community engagement (652 points, 233 comments). The piece is expert commentary rather than a technical breakthrough, but it draws on a well-documented record: the NSA's Stellar Wind program conducted warrantless surveillance under Bush's President's Surveillance Program, and Section 702 (enacted in 2008) still permits bulk collection targeting foreigners that inevitably sweeps in Americans' communications. Schneier's core argument is that these powers have persisted despite weak evidence of security benefit and substantial harm to civil liberties.

**Background**: After the September 11, 2001 attacks, the US dramatically expanded surveillance: the NSA's Stellar Wind program began warrantless monitoring under the President's Surveillance Program, and efforts like PRISM later pulled data directly from major technology companies' servers. In 2008, Congress codified much of this activity in FISA Section 702, which authorizes targeting foreigners abroad but inevitably collects Americans' communications in the process. Edward Snowden's 2013 disclosures made these programs public and triggered a global privacy debate, yet many of the underlying authorities have been repeatedly renewed. The 25th anniversary gives critics like Schneier a natural moment to argue the experiment has run long enough.
